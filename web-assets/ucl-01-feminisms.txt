Thinking with Feminisms:
Reflections on Ballet, Binaries, and Beyond

Introduction

Growing up immersed in ballet from age four, a world traditionally allied to femininity,
it epitomized the essence of being feminine—delicate, emotional, and deeply intuitive.
This paper explores how varied experiences oriented around ballet shaped my
understanding of feminist ideas and societal structures. It introduced my journey of
thinking about feminism into three stages and offered me a trigger to reflect on
feminism and my change in being. Beginning with gendered expectations in ballet,
analysis at each stage relates to various feminist perspectives such as gender binary,
gender performance, zero-sum issues, intersectionality, and types of feminism.
However, throughout the writing process, I recognized that my journey toward
embracing feminism was not triggered by a single event but rather a series of
introspective challenges, underscoring the circuitous nature of this reflective journey.
This tortuous process, in turn, mirrors the development of social-scoped feminism.
Lastly, the paper discusses my gradual reconciliation with the feminist identity, or more
precisely, my self-positioning within feminist discourse. This progress in adopting the
feminist label, as Yoder, Tobias, and Snell (2010) assert, is more crucial than mere
awareness of its importance.

The First Offer: Gender Performance and Binaries

I remember, years ago, the feeling of joy mixed with surprise when I learned my ballet
peer, Anna, was accepted into the prestigious engineering program at UC Berkeley. This
instance is a pivotal moment that taught me to challenge gender roles, especially the
femininity assumed by ballet versus the masculinity assumed by logic and rationality
in a field like engineering and the entire science field.

Moving from China to the UK for undergraduate studies, I encountered a sociopolitical
climate rich in feminist discourse (Guillard 2016), especially on social media,
dramatically reshaping my understanding of knowledge and gender relations
(Greenhow and Lewin 2016). This environment, coupled with my major in the social
sciences, which often deals with gender issues, has enabled me to expand and augment
my understanding of gender roles. These exposures have moved me beyond the
superficial binary perspective of gender, which sees ' male' and 'female' as mutually
exclusive, a notion deeply embedded in societal expectations. I began to question my
surprise at Anna's transition from ballet to the technical and logical engineering world
and whether it was my internalization of these expectations and constructs, especially
regarding the segregation of professions and interests. Since then, I have always
considered individual experiences and dilemmas regarding their roots in social and
political structures (Hanisch 1969).

3

It is this engagement with the concept of gender performativity that has unravelled the
gender binary. Judith Butler (1990) theorized that gender identities and roles are not
innate but are constituted and brought to life through repetitive social enactments, thus
portraying gender as a dynamic and diverse practice rather than a binary division. This
perspective suggests that attributes like rationality and emotionality are supposed to be
detached from their traditional gender associations. 'Rational woman' is, from a feminist
point of view, not so much a contradiction in terms but as a normal situation
(Prokhovnik 2012). In essence, Anna's move from a domain marked by its femininity
to a field deemed masculine is not a transition but a coexistence or reconciliation. This
unsettles binary gendered narratives and the deeply but invisibly interconnected
normative relations of ballet, sensibility, and femininity, as well as engineering,
rationality, and masculinity.

The Second Offer: Challenging Zero-Sum Gender Dynamics

Upon Anna's graduation and subsequent offer from a tech company, our reunion in
London brought another revelation for me. As she spoke of leveraging coding in her
job, I expressed admiration, confessing that my hesitation in coding stemmed from a
self-perceived lack of rationality and mediocrity in mathematics. Anna interjected,
"Wait, that's a common misconception. Coding isn't like what you think." At that time,
deeply immersed in feminist utopian literature, I had an epiphany that my fundamental
misconception lay not in coding but in my narrow imagination of myself, women, and

4

gender roles and capabilities. This flawed perception was the deep-seated barrier to my
grasp of coding. Despite being well-versed in feminist discourse and aware of the
growing presence of women in STEM, integrating these inspirations consistently in
everyday situations remained a challenge.

At this stage, my reflection centred on how patriarchy limits our imaginative capacity,
as underscored in discussions of feminist utopias. The gender binary, I realized,
enforced a zero-sum dynamic, where one gender's gain is seen as another's loss (HareMustin 1987). In other words, any gains toward equality made by women "cost" men,
creating obstacles to gender equity (Mayer 2017; Ruthig et al. 2016). In contrast,
feminist thought illuminates the interrelatedness between reason and emotion, man and
woman (Prokhovnik 2012). It challenges the patriarchal apprehension that women's
achievements come at men's expense, instead proposing that gender equity yields
benefits for society as a whole.

Moreover, the idea that it is the synergy between patriarchy and capitalism that
diminishes women's labour force (Hartmann 1976) enlightened me on the
undervaluation of "female traits" such as intuition and relationship-building. Seen as
mere concessions to male dominance (Gilligan 1982), these traits are mistakenly
deemed inferior within the patriarchal zero-sum game. Such erroneous generalizations
wrongly suggest women lack the rationality for scientific pursuits or the principles for
daily life (Eisenstein 1988), illustrating a more profound issue where "female traits" are

discounted due to a misguided patriarchal bias.

"The personal is political" underscores the connection between individual experiences
and broader societal and political contexts (Hanisch 1969). Anna's realization that
structural barriers prevent women from exercising autonomy and fulfilling their
potential (Yoong 2018) inspired me to reassess the socio-political underpinnings of
individual struggles, not to back down but with a transformative perspective and the
audacity to resist. Confronted with how patriarchal standards dictate "appropriate" for
women (Adisa, Abdulraheem, and Isiaka 2019), I introspected on the gender-based
educational segregation entrenched by societal expectations and perpetuated by
institutions like schools, family units, and media (Archer et al. 2020; Gupta and Keenan
2022; Institute of Physics 2012, 2020). This novel understanding of the rationale and
consequences of the gender binary led to a reexamination of my educational decisions,
especially my forsaken pursuit of architectural design, a decision based on the belief
that I lacked math skills and logical thinking despite being competent in physics.
Further, the realization that an imaginative, non-binary approach could redefine
measures of academic achievement (Gupta and Keenan 2022) echoes that dismantling
gender binaries promises a richer collective bounty that defies the zero-sum
assumptions inherent in patriarchal gender roles.

The Third Offer: Embracing Intersectionality and Activism

6

Anna's recent update about our mutual ballet friend, Irene's acceptance to the
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, unleashed much thought on my part rather than
surprise, sharply contrasting with the amazement I used to feel stemmed from a once
narrow perception of gender and a limited view of women's potential.

This shift from "how remarkable she is as a girl who dances ballet with me" to only
concentrating on "how outstanding she is." I owe it to the systematic learning of
feminist ways of thinking, which resonated with feminist ideas encountered in a school
module. The lens of intersectionality has allowed me to focus more sharply on how
multiple identities and modes of oppression uniquely affect individual experiences.
This notion moves beyond the constrained 'boy discourse' through which differences
are often analyzed without considering other dimensions (Lahelma 2014). The mantra
"the personal is political" empowers individuals, especially women, to recognize and
dismantle structural oppression, while intersectionality provides the tools to challenge
monolithic narratives. A comprehensive strategy that, taken together, subverts
synergistically entrenched oppression that underpins patriarchal structures. Hanisch,
the pioneering voice in coining "the personal is political," further elaborated this in
2006: individual actions embody the broader societal struggle, especially as oppression
often occurs in isolated contexts.

On the other hand, the struggle at the individual level is circumscribed; it is collective

7

activism over the long haul that can contest toxic patriarchy and violent masculinities
(Patel 2021). Thus, Individual resistance reflects society's structures and is part of a
larger tapestry of societal change. Social structural wrongs cause personal suffering, but
feminist activists empower every individual, not just women.

The framework of intersectionality, especially when aligned with postfeminist theories
that emphasize the dynamic and diversity of identities, enables a holistic view of how
identity and oppression intersect and affect our experience (Crenshaw 1991; Gill 2016)
and allows us to "perform a beneficial, therapeutical form of self-socio-analysis and
build literacy regarding their own complex social position in the world" (Collins et al.
2021, p. 714).

Our shared background in ballet, a symbol of elite status, simultaneously guided our
early gender performativity. Despite these commonalities, our individual experiences
and progress in feminist awakening diverge significantly. Anna and Irene diverged from
gender perception earlier than I did, successfully navigating STEM fields—a testament
to their ability to recognize, challenge, and transcend gender norms. This underscores
that their achievements stem not just from aptitude but from a critical consciousness
that questioned gender roles—a realization that eluded me when I doubted my
mathematical skills, mistakenly attributing it to a lack of ability. Above all, I never
honestly expected myself to excel in the sciences.

8

Considering intersectional differences develops a more thorough cross-cultural analysis
(Connell 2009; Davis 2008), such as national variances (Dosekun 2015). In this case,
Anna and Irene, raised in the United States, experienced a cultural diversity not
available to me in China. Zooming into gender debates, Mohanty (1988), in "Under
Western Eyes," identifies differences between the East and West in the micropolitics of
context, subjectivity, struggle, and the macropolitics of global economic and political
systems. Neoliberal feminism underlines how market mechanisms drive gender
equality. This viewpoint helps explain how higher middle-class women capitalized on
the more outstanding educational and employment opportunities to break gender
occupational segregation, eventually penetrating the upper echelons of power structures
within a field that is traditionally male-dominated (Connell 2009; Fraser 2009;
Hartmann 1976).

In employing neoliberal feminist perspectives, I strive to recognize Anna's
achievements without reducing them solely to individual choice. I remain cognizant of
the structural issues and collective actions highlighted by post-feminism and
intersectionality (McRobbie, 2009). It is exhausting yet rewarding to reconcile
contradictions across multiple perspectives in search of my stance. Also, my privilege
within Chinese society, that of being able to receive an education overseas and being
able to begin ballet at an early age, requires that I recognize my empowering
experiences are not accessible to many marginalized Chinese women. I question
whether they can practice or even envisage such empowered ways of being. Critiques

9

of neoliberalism validate my concerns, as only women with sufficient resources and
opportunities in the market can secure tangible advancements (Rottenberg 2018), while
those facing more obstacles and unable to compete in the market are left out.

Conclusion

My overall journey of thinking with feminism has been akin to the process of losing fat.
Recognizing the need to shed ‘mental fat'—unlearn gender stereotypes—we adopt a
comprehensive approach through broad feminist education, analogous to overall
aerobic efforts essential for full-body fat loss. Despite being familiar with seminal
feminist texts such as Woolf's "A Room of One's Own" (1929) and De Beauvoir's "The
Second Sex" (1949) during my high school years, integrating these ideas into my daily
consciousness was challenging—I still felt surprised at Anna's offer due to her
association with ballet. However, through iterative learning and recognizing more
profound cognitive limitations, I overcame them with feminist insight. Thinking with
feminism is an uncomfortable unravelling process where activists break habitual
thinking patterns (Gill 2016), confront contradictions in gender paradigms, and seek to
influence society through sharing personal narratives, just as post-feminism participates
in the deconstruction of feminism (McRobbie 2004). The enlightening exchanges
among women snowball into public discourse, generating broader awareness. Anna
guided me by sharing insights highlighting the importance of connecting personal
experiences with broader societal and political structures (Lee 2007)—evidence of

Anna's active involvement in the women's rights movement (Swank and Fahs 2017).
Having feminist friends like Anna during adolescence, as noted by Smith and Payne
(2015), provides exposure to feminist discourse, preventing missed opportunities to
disrupt gender bias and enrich feminism's imagination.

These three offers have indeed offered me opportunities to dive into feminism.
Understanding the gender binary revealed social structures, while gender performance
highlighted core issues, and the rejection of zero-sum thinking deconstructed
patriarchal limits on imagination. Embracing intersectionality and the notion that "the
personal is political" has shown me how every advance helps to break down complex
gender, class, and power dynamics, working towards a more progressive society.
Initially, writing reflective essays seemed daunting due to potential biases, but I've
come to see my perspective as a source of meaningful contributions to specific
discussions. Cultivated by the UK's feminist environment, exposure to feminist
literature, and guidance from feminist' friends, my discomfort signifies my active
engagement with feminism—questioning and challenging established thoughts and
actions.
Most recently, I've made the decision to pursue architectural design during my
postgraduate studies, ready to tackle the required advanced mathematics and mechanics.
Once hesitant to identify as a feminist, my journey through various narratives, academic
engagement, and activism, as well as witnessing societal constraints on personal
potential, has cumulatively assembled my feminist standing. What once felt distant is

now integral to my identity—I am now confident in considering myself a feminist.
